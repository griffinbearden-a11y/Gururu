Two. Two weeks, two wins, and two distinct classes of delusion operating simultaneously across twelve rosters that insist on treating schedule assignment as an absolute measure of structural competence rather than a temporary artifact of probability.

When a manager observes an unblemished record after fourteen days of competition, the human impulse is to construct an elaborate narrative regarding roster construction, waiver wire acuity, and tactical foresight, as though the universe paused its operations to reward clever management. This instinct, while psychologically comforting, fundamentally misunderstands the mechanics of small-sample variance in head-to-head competition, where a team can accumulate victories simply by stepping onto the field against an opponent who happened to experience an uncharacteristically depressed scoring output on that specific Sunday.

A two-win designation in a twelve-team league at this precise juncture carries a standard error so broad that drawing conclusions from it borders on statistical malpractice, yet the league insists on treating the standings table as a moral leaderboard. To evaluate these records properly, one must strip away the emotional residue of the box scores and examine the underlying point differentials, because points scored and points allowed remain the only stable inputs available while weekly matchups behave like rolling dice.
