One point five years is the structural deficit by which the career window of a running back trails every comparable offensive position, a mathematical reality that the managerial collective in this league continues to treat as an inconvenient suggestion rather than an actuarial boundary. When the position's peak compresses and its collapse arrives with virtually no warning, treating a disposable roster acquisition as a durable cornerstone is not optimism, but rather a fundamental misunderstanding of baseline probability.

Managers across this twelve-team ecosystem routinely misapprehend this depreciation curve because they conflate recent volume with long-term asset retention. A running back who happens to inherit a temporary share of backfield touches, much like the high-usage precedents set by elite assets such as Jonathan Taylor or Jahmyr Gibbs, is evaluated on his ceiling week rather than his actuarial floor. The market consistently prices these players as though their production exists in a vacuum, immune to the physical toll that turns efficiency cliffs into vertical drops.

This analytical failure becomes particularly acute during the late-summer transactional window, where minor roster adjustments and waiver claims are greeted with the kind of reverence usually reserved for foundational draft capital. To expend finite capital or managerial energy on assets whose market half-life is measured in months rather than seasons is to misunderstand the mechanics of dynasty asset management. The historical record, encompassing peak profiles ranging from Bijan Robinson to countless predecessors who vanished from relevance by their late twenties, demonstrates quite plainly that running backs drafted or acquired outside elite consensus tiers do not appreciate in value.

That several rosters have spent the preceding weeks allocating resources to secondary backfields suggests the lesson has once again been bypassed in favor of emotional narrative. When an asset's expected return decays at a rate faster than its acquisition cost can be amortized, the trade or waiver claim is mathematically net-negative regardless of how the manager feels about their depth on a Tuesday in September. The numbers governing positional life cycles are fixed, and while ignoring them requires considerable effort, the eventual reconciliation with the waiver wire is entirely unnegotiable.
